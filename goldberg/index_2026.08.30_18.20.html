Phase 1 · Sessions 1-8

Orientation

You just got hired as a Kinetic Engineer at Goldberg's Novelty Installations, and before anyone hands you a blueprint, you have to earn it the hard way — building twelve real mechanisms with your own hands and figuring out, just by watching them move, which kind of math secretly controls each one.

What You'll Produce

Function-Mechanism Orientation Log

Your personal record of testing all 12 mechanisms and matching each one to the function type that best explains how it moves.

What it needs to include
  • Build or set up all 12 items from the fixed list and physically test each one.
  • Match each item to quadratic, exponential decay, rational/inverse variation, or trigonometric — or note that none fit cleanly.
  • Back up every match with real evidence you observed while testing, without using formulas or vocabulary yet.
  • Include at least one match you revised after further testing, and explain what changed your mind.
  • Fill out the Orientation Log template with all 12 items entered as separate rows.

Phase 2 · Sessions 9-28

Certification

Everything you noticed by hand in Orientation is about to get a name and a formula — and Goldberg's won't let you anywhere near your actual machine section until you're certified, exactly like a real engineer proving they're licensed for the job.

What You'll Produce

Function Type Certifications

Two passed certification tests proving you have the math skills to design your own section of the machine.

What it needs to include
  • Complete instruction on quadratic functions and equations and score 80% or higher on the Quadratics Certification Test.
  • Complete instruction on exponential decay, rational/inverse variation, and trigonometric functions and score 80% or higher on the combined Certification Test.
  • Get certified on both tests before Phase 3 (Design) begins.
  • If you score below 80% on a test, complete remediation and pass a retest.

Phase 3 · Sessions 29-43

Design

You're certified — now you get to measure the actual foyer wall, claim your motto word, and design a section of a real machine that has to physically hand off to your teammates' sections, all before staff will sign off on letting you build it.

What You'll Produce

Section Design Package

Your individual design document proving your section will work mathematically, mechanically, and thematically, presented to staff for design sign-off.

What it needs to include
  • Use at least three different function types, each backed by a written design-check calculation.
  • Use at least four different simple machine components.
  • Include a to-scale diagram of your section's layout on your peg boards.
  • Define your section's incoming and outgoing trigger tolerance zones so they match your teammates' handoff points.
  • Explain how your section's design embodies your assigned motto word.
  • Include a materials list with estimated costs against your share of the $200 budget.
  • Present your design orally to staff and collect their feedback.
  • Revise your package based on staff feedback and resubmit for sign-off.

Build

Your design is approved — now you actually build it, test it against your own math, argue with reality when it doesn't match, and get your section mounted permanently where prospective families will trigger it on tours for years to come.

What You'll Produce

Installed Machine Section

Your physically built, permanently mounted section of the machine, reliable enough for daily use and ready to hand off to your teammates' sections.

What it needs to include
  • Build a working prototype of your section and test its real performance against your math predictions.
  • Troubleshoot and adjust until your section runs successfully in at least 9 of 10 trials.
  • Run a handoff checkpoint with your neighboring engineer to confirm your trigger interface actually works.
  • Mount your section onto the shared plywood board in its assigned spot and demo it live for staff.
  • Write a one-page maintenance and reset instruction sheet for a non-engineer.
  • Finalize your materials cost log and your design/construction files.
  • Revise your section to address staff feedback before final installation.

Your Role, In The Field
Kinetic Engineer

A real kinetic engineer spends the morning measuring a physical space down to the inch, then the afternoon running the same test over and over, adjusting one small thing each time until a trigger lands exactly where it needs to. There's a lot of math on paper before anything gets built, and a lot of troubleshooting once it does — friction and material quirks never show up in the calculation until you're holding the actual part in your hands.

You're individually responsible for designing, engineering, and building one section of the shared machine, representing one word of the motto: Create, Cultivate, or Collaborate. Your section has to physically interface with your teammates' sections and trigger the next one in the chain — which means every decision you make about timing, materials, or placement is also a decision you're making with them.

Your First Commission: The Foyer Installation
Welcome to Goldberg's Novelty Installations. You're on the team now, which means you're a Kinetic Engineer — someone who designs machines that move, trigger, and hand off to each other without failing. Your first commission is a big one, and it's permanent. The school has hired us to build a wall-mounted Rube Goldberg machine for their foyer. It needs three connected sections, each one representing a word from the school's motto: Create, Cultivate, Collaborate. When it's finished, it goes up for good — and it has to run reliably, day after day, for prospective families touring the building. Not once. Not sometimes. Every time. Before anyone touches a peg board, you're going to spend time in the shop, building and testing the kinds of mechanisms real installations depend on — pendulums, ramps, levers, springs. Watch how they move. You'll need that evidence later, when the math gets formal and you're certified to work on your section. Once you're certified, you'll survey the actual foyer, design your section against real wall measurements, and build something that has to hand off perfectly to your teammates' sections. School staff will review your work at every major checkpoint — this has to be good enough to stand in that foyer permanently. Welcome aboard.
— M. Voss, Director of Installations
